Ephesus is one of the best-preserved ancient cities anywhere on the Mediterranean — and, thanks to its position near the cruise port of Kuşadası, one of the most crowded sites in Turkey by mid-morning. This guide gives you the route that keeps you ahead of the tour buses and tells you what's actually worth the walk.

## The One Decision That Matters: Which Gate You Enter

Ephesus has two entrances — an upper gate and a lower gate — connected by a gently downhill marble street. Cruise ship groups overwhelmingly enter through the **lower gate** mid-morning and walk uphill, because it's closer to the coach parking. That means the single best move available to you is simple:

**Enter through the upper gate, right at opening, and walk downhill.**

You'll pass most tour groups coming the other way, get the marble street and the Library of Celsus largely to yourself in the first hour, and finish downhill instead of climbing at the end of your visit.

## The Route, Upper Gate to Lower Gate

**The State Agora and Odeon.** Just inside the upper entrance — a former civic square and a small theater used for council meetings and performances, a quiet, uncrowded start to the walk.

**Curetes Street.** The main downhill avenue, lined with the remains of temples, fountains and shops, including the Temple of Hadrian — one of the best-preserved smaller monuments on site, with detailed relief carvings still visible on its arch.

**The Terrace Houses (optional, separate ticket).** Roman-era homes of Ephesus's wealthier residents, excavated and preserved under a protective roof, with mosaic floors and painted walls still in place. It costs extra and takes real time, but it's the closest thing to seeing how people in Ephesus actually lived, rather than just the public monuments.

**The Library of Celsus.** The centerpiece, and rightly so — a two-story marble facade from around 117 AD, built as a monumental tomb and library combined, with four statues representing wisdom, knowledge, intelligence and virtue set into its niches. This is the image everyone has of Ephesus before they arrive; seeing it with the crowds still thin, right after opening, is the single best reason to do this route in this direction.

**The Great Theater.** A 25,000-seat Roman theater carved into the hillside, still used occasionally for performances today, and the site's other major landmark — walk to the top tiers for a view back down the length of the ancient city toward the old harbor (now silted up and well inland).

**Exit through the lower gate**, past the Marble Street and Harbor Street — by now filling with the groups you passed hours earlier going the other way.

## Beyond the Main Site

**The House of the Virgin Mary**, a small stone chapel in the hills above Ephesus, is a significant pilgrimage site believed by some traditions to be where Mary lived her final years — worth the short taxi ride if it interests you, though it's a separate stop from the main ruins.

**Selçuk town**, a few minutes away, has the Ephesus Archaeological Museum (home to many of the site's smaller finds and statues, including pieces too delicate to leave outdoors), the ruins of the Temple of Artemis — once one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, now reduced to a single reconstructed column — and the Basilica of St. John. It's a reasonable base if you're not day-tripping from Kuşadası or Izmir.

## Getting There

Ephesus sits inland from the cruise port of Kuşadası (about 20–30 minutes) and close to Izmir (roughly an hour). Cruise passengers typically arrive as part of a ship excursion, mid-to-late morning — exactly the window this guide's route is designed to avoid. Independent travelers based in Selçuk or Kuşadası have the real advantage here: nothing stops you from being at the upper gate the moment it opens.

## Three Ephesus Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Entering through the lower gate mid-morning.** It's the default for most visitors and exactly why the site feels overwhelming by 10 a.m.
2. **Skipping the Terrace Houses to save money.** They cost extra, but they're the difference between seeing monuments and seeing how people actually lived — worth it if history is any part of why you came.
3. **Underestimating the sun.** The site has almost no shade across the marble street and agoras; bring water and a hat regardless of season.
